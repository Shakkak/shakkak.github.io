Borrow, then personalize

How I tried to predict a new person’s brain responses from a few hundred images, and what I learned on the way

The question

When you look at a picture, your brain answers with a pattern. We can record it with EEG (sensors on the head) or with fMRI (a scanner). An encoding model takes the image and predicts this pattern. It works on features from a pretrained image network (AlexNet in the main tests, as in the THINGS-EEG2 paper), not on raw pixels.

A good model for one person needs many hours of their recordings. A new person usually gives much less, maybe a few hundred labelled images. But we already have models of many other people. So my question was simple:

If I have only a few hundred images from a new person, what is the best way to use them, when models of other people already exist?

First, what the data looks like

Everything later in this post is easier if you know what one piece of data is. So let me show it first.

In all five datasets the setup is similar. A person sits and looks at many photos of objects and scenes, one after another, while a machine records their brain. Each photo gives one brain response. One photo together with this person’s response to it is what I call a labelled image. A model learns from these pairs.

I took the test part of the data (the 200 test photos with their 80 repeats, see the THINGS-EEG2 card above) for all 10 people, and made small averages from it.

A real response. Figure 1 shows the signal of one sensor at the back of the head (Oz, above the visual cortex) for five photos. Time 0 is the moment the photo appears. In this dataset the photos come very fast, a new one every 200 ms, so the line is never flat: you also see the waves of the photos before and after, and that makes the regular ripple. The response to this photo is where the five coloured lines split apart, most clearly in the grey band, 100 to 200 ms. That early response is what I scored in the EEG tests. Pick another person from the menu: the general shape stays, but the details move.

One photo, 80 times. The five coloured lines in Figure 1 are averages over 80 recordings each. In Figure 2 each thin grey line is one of the 80 repeats: one time that this person saw the cat photo, and the thick green line is their average. You can almost not see the response in a single line, but the average shows it clearly. Keep this in mind: later it explains why the method works on some datasets and not on others.

Are two people similar? This is the key question for the whole project. If two people answer the same photos in the same way, we can borrow from one to predict the other. Here is how I measured it, step by step:

  1. One person’s pattern. For one person, take the response to each of the 200 test photos: 17 sensors × 100 time points per photo. So the pattern is a big table of numbers that says how this person answered every photo.
  2. Keep only what is special about each photo. All photos share a common wave (the dashed grey line in Figure 1). I subtract it, so what is left is how each photo is different from the others. That is the part an encoding model must predict.
  3. Two halves. I split the 80 repeats into two halves: repeats 1 to 40 and repeats 41 to 80. Each half gives its own pattern, made from different recordings, so each half has its own noise.
  4. Compare with a correlation. 1 means the two patterns are the same, 0 means they have nothing in common.
    • Same person: first half against second half of the same person. This is the best we can hope for, because even one person does not match themself perfectly: the noise is different in each half.
    • Two people: the first half of one person against the second half of another person. I use halves here too, so both comparisons have the same amount of noise and are fair to each other.

Figure 3 shows all these comparisons. The row is the person of the first half, the column the person of the second half. The squares on the diagonal compare a person with themself; all other squares compare two people.

In Figure 3, the squares on the diagonal are darker than the rest. On average, two halves of the same person are similar at 0.39, and two different people at 0.15. This is not zero: people respond to the same photos in a similar way. That is why borrowing from other people works at all. But it is clearly lower than the same person: each person also has their own part. That is the part a personal map should learn.

NoteFor ML readers: the data as a regression problem

Every dataset in this project turns into the same supervised problem. An image goes in as a feature vector, a vector of brain outputs comes out, and there is one model per person. What changes from dataset to dataset is how long that output vector is and how noisy each target is, and those two things end up deciding most of the results.

The input is always a 1,000-dimensional vector: AlexNet features compressed by kernel PCA (the method box in the next section has the details). These features never see brain data. The output depends on the recording. For EEG it is 17 sensors × 100 time points, so 1,700 numbers per image. The epochs are whitened per person or per session by multivariate noise normalisation, as in the THINGS-EEG2 paper, which is why Figure 1 has no unit. For NSD it is 39,161 vertices on the fsaverage template surface, the ones common to all eight people, z-scored within each scanning session and averaged over an image’s repeats. For NOD it is a released single-trial beta map, scored on the 14,733 vertices that the donor models predict above a small threshold.

The asymmetry between labels and test targets matters more than it looks. A labelled image’s target is a single trial in THINGS-EEG1 and NOD, the mean of 4 trials in THINGS-EEG2 and Alljoined, and the mean of up to 3 in NSD. The test targets are far cleaner. In THINGS-EEG2, following the dataset’s own code, each prediction is correlated with the mean of a random half of the 80 repeats, and this is averaged over 100 random halves. In THINGS-EEG2 the training and test photos also share no object concept, so the test is about new kinds of objects, not new photos of known ones.

How clean a target can be is captured by the noise ceiling. If an output has signal variance \(s^2\) across images and trial noise variance \(n^2\), the best correlation any model can reach against an average of \(m\) repeats is

\[\rho_m = \sqrt{\frac{s^2}{s^2 + n^2/m}}.\]

More repeats give a higher ceiling. This is the formal version of the diagonal in Figure 3, and later in the post the “room” a method has to improve is this ceiling minus what borrowing already reaches.

Figure 3 is computed in a simple way. For each person and each half of the repeats, the responses form a 200 × 17 × 100 array. I subtract the mean over the 200 images, flatten the rest, and correlate one person’s first half with another person’s second half. Subtracting the mean matters: it removes the average waveform of Figure 1, which every photo shares and which would make everyone look alike. What is left is the part that tells photos apart, and that is exactly what an encoding model is scored on.

With this in mind, the rest of the post is the story of how I found that borrowing is a strong start, and that a small personal map on top of it helps when the person’s labelled images are clean enough.

Where I started: a big shared network

Steps 1 to 3 of 8Idea, reproduce first, looks good

At the start I followed the trend in the field. Many recent papers train one network on many people and add a small part for each person. My plan was the same: one shared network for everyone, and a small extra part trained for each new person.

Before building anything, I reproduced a published EEG encoding model and its baselines, and checked that my numbers matched the paper. This step was slow and boring, but later it saved me. Every new idea was compared with baselines that I knew were correct.

On THINGS-EEG2 (ten people) the shared network looked good. With 250 or 500 images from a new person it beat the baselines. I was happy. But there was a problem: I had used these same ten people while building the model. A model can look good on the people it was tuned on. Work like this, where I can look at the data and try things, is what I call development. It is useful for finding ideas, but it does not prove them.

The test that changed the project

Step 4 of 8The real test

So I wrote the test down before running it. A protocol is a short file where I wrote, before the run, what I would measure and which result would count as a success. I saved it in git, so the order (protocol first, results later) is on record. It fixed the model, the people, the score, and the margin: the smallest gain I agreed to call real, and not luck. Then I ran the test once, on 24 people of a second EEG dataset (THINGS-EEG1) that no model had seen. A test written down like this before the run is called prespecified.

The score is encoding accuracy, written r: the correlation between the predicted response and the real one, in the early response (100 to 200 ms, the grey band in Figure 1). EEG is noisy, so real values are small, around 0.1 to 0.2.

NoteFor ML readers: how a test decides

A test here never asks “is the mean gain positive?”. It asks whether the gain is larger than the margin, given that only 8 to 24 people are available. That makes it closer to a non-inferiority or superiority trial in medicine than to the usual “beat the baseline on the benchmark”.

The unit is the person. For each new person I take the method’s score minus the comparator’s, averaged over the two label budgets (k = 250 and 500) and over three fixed random draws of the k images. Over the n people, a one-sided t bound with n − 1 degrees of freedom is computed at α = 0.05 divided by the number of claims in the protocol, so the Bonferroni correction is within a protocol, not across protocols. The verdict then has three possible values:

  • supported if the lower bound is above the margin;
  • rejected if the upper bound is below it;
  • inconclusive otherwise.

A test with several comparators is supported only if every one of them clears. That rule is what made THINGS-EEG2 “not sure”, as a later section shows.

The margins came from different places. On THINGS-EEG1 and THINGS-EEG2 the margin is 5% of the room between borrowing and a full own model (0.0035 and 0.011). Alljoined uses the THINGS-EEG2 margin scaled by its people’s split-half reliability (0.0021). NSD’s comes from its released noise ceilings (0.030 in correlation, 2 points of identification), and NOD’s from nine development people (0.011 and 0.5 points). They are a convention I set, not external thresholds, and they were not always fixed from data outside the test.

The bound has a known weakness. The people share test images and, partly, donors, so they are not fully independent draws. A bootstrap over images as well as people left the supported NSD claims above their margins, which is reassuring but not a full answer. Finally, each protocol was committed to a private git repository before its test was scored. The protocols and their commit identifiers are released with the code, but the history itself is not public, so this is not a public registration.

The shared network beat the person’s own model: a model trained only on the person’s own few hundred labelled images (250 or 500; the charts show the average of the two). But it lost to something much simpler: just average the predictions of the models of other people. I call this borrowing. The test said “rejected”, and I kept that result.

In Figure 4, use the menu above the chart to switch between the two comparisons. Each line is one person. A coloured line means the method on the right was better for this person; a grey line means the method on the left was better. Hover a point to see the person.

In Figure 4, borrowing was better than the person’s own model for 22 of 24 people. The shared network was below borrowing for 18 of 24.

This was the most important moment of the project. I had spent a lot of time on the big network, and a plain average of other people beat it, on people it had never seen. If I had not written the test first, I could have searched for some setting where the network wins, and I would have believed it.

NoteFor ML readers: why a plain average wins here

It is a bias–variance story. The new person’s own model has to learn 1,000 × 1,700 = 1.7 million weights from a few hundred noisy single trials, so its variance is huge, even with ridge regression and a penalty chosen per output by five-fold cross-validation. Borrowing has no variance from the new person at all. Its error is pure bias: the gap between “other people on average” and this person. With a few hundred single trials, that bias is smaller than the own model’s variance, and on THINGS-EEG1 borrowing beat the own model by 0.050, for 22 of 24 people.

The shared network sat in between. It was trained jointly across the donors, on the same fixed features, with a personal read-out for each person. It scored 0.0075 below borrowing (18 of 24 lower), and the protocol rejected it. A follow-up then tried to rescue the network’s representation by using it as a correction on top of borrowing. It gained +0.0087 for 21 of 24 people, but its lower bound (0.0032) missed the 0.0035 margin.

The trade-off moves with the labels. When each label is an average of repeats, the own model’s variance drops and it can win. On NSD (up to 3 repeats per label) the own model reached 0.275 and 0.308 at 250 and 500 images, against borrowing’s 0.251, and on THINGS-EEG2 (4 repeats) it reached 0.374 against 0.342 at 500. These comparisons were not prespecified, but they set the scope: “borrowing is the better start” is a claim about single-trial labels.

Borrow, then personalize

Steps 5 to 6 of 8Borrow, then personalize

So I changed the direction. If borrowing is such a strong start, the new person’s few hundred images should not build a model from zero. They should correct the borrowed prediction.

The correction is small on purpose. It is a linear map that mixes the borrowed prediction across EEG sensors (or fMRI brain spots) in the way this one person needs. It is regularised, so a few hundred images are enough to fit it. I call it the personal map. Two steps:

  1. Borrow. Average the predictions of models trained on other people. No labels of the new person needed. This works because everyone’s data is in the same layout: the same 17 sensor positions for EEG, and the same template brain surface for fMRI, so “brain spot number 1,000” is the same place in every person.
  2. Personalize. Fit the small map from the new person’s labelled images, on top of that average.
NoteFor ML readers: the method, step by step

The whole method is linear. Borrowing gives a prediction with no labels. The map is a small regression that predicts what borrowing got wrong for this person, using borrowing’s own output as input. It is shrunk towards “no correction”, so with too few or too noisy labels it falls back to plain borrowing instead of making things worse.

Features and donors. The features are the THINGS-EEG2 paper’s: eight AlexNet layers, concatenated and standardised, a degree-4 polynomial kernel and kernel PCA to 1,000 components, fitted on images only. The DINOv2 (ViT-B/14) variant pools the patch tokens of layers 4, 8 and 12 to a 7 × 7 grid before the same kernel PCA. Each donor \(d\) has one linear encoder \(f_d\) from features to every output: least squares, as in the THINGS-EEG2 paper, except on NOD, where least squares overfits single trials and ridge is used instead.

Borrowing is the plain mean of the donors’ predictions, with no weights:

\[\bar y(x) = \frac{1}{D}\sum_{d=1}^{D} f_d(x).\]

The donors are every other person in the dataset (9 in THINGS-EEG2, 15 in Alljoined, 7 in NSD), or a fixed development group (22 people in THINGS-EEG1, 9 in NOD).

The EEG map works in each 100 ms window \(w\). A 17 × 17 matrix \(M_w\) mixes the borrowed prediction across sensors:

\[\hat y(x,t) = \bar y(x,t) + M_w\,\bigl(\bar y(x,t) - \bar y_{\text{test}}(t)\bigr).\]

\(M_w\) is a ridge solution with no intercept, fitted on the k labelled images, after centring both sides, to the residual: the response minus the borrowed prediction. Ten windows of 289 entries make about 2,900 parameters, against 1.7 million for the own model. The centring keeps borrowing’s offset, and a constant offset does not change a correlation across images.

The low-rank map, used for fMRI and tried on EEG, projects the borrowed prediction on its top \(K = 200\) principal directions \(B\) and regresses the residual on those 200 numbers at every output:

\[\hat y(x) = \bar y(x) + W^{\top} B^{\top}\bigl(\bar y(x) - \mu\bigr) + c.\]

\(B\) comes from borrowed predictions for the person’s labelled images, never from responses. The map is small in its inputs, not its outputs: on NSD it is 200 × 39,161 coefficients, one 200-input ridge per vertex. Because the donors are linear in the same features, there is a neat way to read it: in fMRI the map is the person’s own model restricted to the subspace the donors’ predictions span, and shrunk towards borrowing. The prior is “you are like the average person”, and the labels only move you inside the space the donors cover. Which map works best depends on the budget. On EEG, below about 200 labelled images a full 17 × 17 map per window was best, and from 250 images one low-rank map over the whole response.

Penalties are all chosen by five-fold cross-validation on the person’s k labelled images (k = 250 or 500 in the tests), minimising held-out squared error: per output for the own model and the low-rank map, one per window for the EEG map, with all time points of an image kept in the same fold. No test image is used anywhere.

The combination with the own model uses, per output \(v\), least-squares weights \(a_{jv}\) on the image-centred predictions of the map and the own model, fitted on their five-fold out-of-fold predictions for the k images:

\[\hat y_v(x) = \sum_j a_{jv}\,\bigl(p_{jv}(x) - \bar p_{jv}\bigr) + \bar r_v,\]

where \(\bar r_v\) is the person’s mean labelled response.

What it had to beat. On THINGS-EEG1 the protocol named six alternatives: borrowing, Gu et al.’s weighted ensemble of donors, a linear converter that maps another person’s responses onto this person, the own model with and without an output map, and the same correction averaged over other people. That last one added nothing, which says the gain needs the person’s own fit, not just “some correction”. Encoding is scored as the correlation across test images at each output, averaged over outputs; identification picks the test image whose prediction correlates best, across outputs, with the measured response, among 200.

Then I did the same as before: protocol first, one run, a fixed margin. On the 24 THINGS-EEG1 people the map improved on borrowing for 23 of them, in the early response. To be honest about it: this follow-up used the same 24 people as the first test, and I wrote it after I saw the first result, so it is weaker evidence than a test on fresh people.

NoteFor ML readers: what the map learns

A small linear map could simply be soaking up noise. A set of development checks on THINGS-EEG1 and THINGS-EEG2 says it learns something stable about the person instead.

First, it is personal and reproducible. A person’s map fitted on images 1–1,000 looks like their map fitted on images 1,001–2,000, while different people’s maps differ. On THINGS-EEG2, maps fitted on each image’s repeats from its earlier and its later recording session have similar sensor mixing (cosine similarity 0.54, against 0.01 between people, at 100–300 ms), and one session’s map picks out each of the ten people among everyone’s maps from the other session, but only after image onset. Both of those maps use the same images, so this shows stability across repeats, not across new photos.

Second, it does not transfer. Another person’s map applied to a new person scored below plain borrowing, for all 22 THINGS-EEG1 development people tested. And it is not a time shift: aligning each person’s response in time first leaves 97% (THINGS-EEG1) and 99.5% (THINGS-EEG2) of the gain, so what the map does is spatial re-mixing.

The gain is also early. It sits at 100–200 ms, the scored window. Over the whole epoch the lead over borrowing shrinks to 0.002 on THINGS-EEG1 and 0.010 on THINGS-EEG2, below the margins. With more donors the personal gain shrinks a little and levels off from about ten.

Classic alignment is the natural comparison. Procrustes-style rotations and a shared-response-style model, fitted on the same labels, also improved on borrowing, but recovered only 22–39% of the map’s gain at 250 images and 35–53% at 500. The difference is shrinkage: they are not pulled towards “no change”, and the rotation lowered accuracy after 400 ms, where the borrowed prediction carries little signal.

Does it hold on other data?

Step 7 of 8Test again

One dataset is not enough. So I wrote new protocols and tested the same idea on new people in three more datasets: a consumer EEG headset, 7-tesla fMRI and single-trial 3-tesla fMRI (each photo recorded only once, with no repeats to average, like one thin grey line in Figure 2). I also repeated the test on THINGS-EEG2, on the ten people I had used before. Each test was run once.

In Figure 5 every dot is one person. Above zero means the map was better than borrowing for that person. The red dashed line is the margin that the protocol said a gain must clear. Next to the dots, the grey mark is the average gain and its bar is the test’s confidence bound (one-sided, corrected for the several comparisons in each test). A test passes when the whole bar is above the margin. Hover a dot for the person, or the grey mark for the numbers.

What Figure 5 says, in short:

  • Lab EEG (THINGS-EEG1 and THINGS-EEG2): the map helped, for almost every person. On THINGS-EEG2 the map beat borrowing by more than the margin, which is what Figure 5 shows. But the protocol asked it to beat six methods by the margin, borrowing and five others, and against two of the others (a weighted average of other people, and a converter that maps another person’s responses onto this person) it fell short. So the THINGS-EEG2 test as a whole is “not sure”. Its people were also the ones I used to build the baselines, so it is the weaker of the two.
  • Consumer EEG (Alljoined, 16 people): the gain went the same way, for 12 of 16 people, but did not clear the margin. The answer here is “not sure”, and I report it like that.
  • 7-tesla fMRI (NSD, 8 people): the map helped every person. Encoding accuracy went from 0.25 with borrowing to about 0.30 with the map, about a fifth higher.
  • Single-trial 3-tesla fMRI (NOD, 21 people): no gain. And this was the result I had predicted in the protocol, before the test. The next section says why I expected it, and what I found later.

Why it works sometimes and not always

Step 8 of 8Why

The pattern became clear from the development work (exploring the data, not prespecified tests). The map can only fix what borrowing leaves on the table. It needs two things:

  1. Room. Noise puts a ceiling on any score. Even a person’s own two halves agree only so much (the diagonal of Figure 3). If borrowing is already close to that ceiling, there is nothing left to gain.
  2. Clean labels. The map learns from the new person’s responses. If each response is very noisy, the map learns noise.

NOD records each image only once, on a 3-tesla scanner, so its labels are very noisy. That is why I predicted no gain there, and the test agreed. But a check I made afterwards showed that my reason was only half right: NOD did leave room, and the map still took very little of it. So the prediction held, but I can’t yet say what limited the map there.

The clean-label part I could check directly on THINGS-EEG2, where each image was shown several times. I trained the map on labels that averaged 1, 2 or 4 repetitions, and kept everything else the same. There are two kinds of map. The full map can mix every sensor with every other sensor. The low-rank map is a smaller version that only works along the 200 main directions of the borrowed prediction; it is the one I used for fMRI, where there are too many brain spots for a full map. In Figure 6, use the menu above the chart to switch between the two maps and two label budgets (250 or 500 labelled images).

Figure 6 shows that with cleaner labels the gain grows, for the same people and the same number of images. So the few hundred images of a new person are worth more when each one is recorded well.

NoteFor ML readers: room, label noise, and where the account breaks

“Room” is the noise ceiling of an output minus borrowing’s accuracy there. Everything below is development analysis, and it is descriptive: reliability, anatomy, how well the features fit, and how good the donors are all vary together, so none of these analyses isolates a cause.

NSD gives the cleanest picture, because it has a ceiling for every vertex. Write \(g\) for the map’s gain at a vertex, \(c\) for its ceiling and \(b\) for borrowing’s accuracy there. Pooled over people’s vertices,

\[g = -0.10 + 0.55\,c - 0.54\,b \qquad (R^2 = 0.63).\]

The two weights are nearly equal, so this is about 0.55 × (room − 0.18): where the room is under about 0.2, the map gains little. One worry is that the gain and borrowing’s accuracy are measured on the same test images, so shared sampling noise could create the negative weight. Measuring borrowing on one half of the images and the gain on the other keeps the loadings (+0.52 and −0.50), so that is not the explanation. On the cortex, the map helps least in primary visual cortex, where borrowing is already good, and most in face, body and word areas. At a typical vertex it takes about an eighth of the room.

On THINGS-EEG2 the 80 test repeats give every sensor and moment a ceiling, and the same relation holds output by output, with room and gain estimated on disjoint halves of the test images (correlation 0.40 to 0.45, positive for all ten people). The slope is smaller, though: about a quarter of each extra unit of room, against a half on NSD. And the timing does not fit: EEG has the most room at about 270 ms, but the map’s gain peaks at 90 to 100 ms. My untested guess is that a re-mixing can only recover what the borrowed prediction reaches, and late in the response it carries little signal.

Label noise is the second half of the account, and Figure 6 tests it directly. For the low-rank map at 500 images, averaging 1, 2 or 4 repeats per label raised the gain from 0.020 to 0.034 to 0.047, or from 6% to 11% to 15% of the room. The labelled images stay the same, so the person records two or four times as many trials. This is the value of cleaner labels, not a free gain.

The account breaks in two places. On NOD, nine development people saw 120 images ten times, which gives a ceiling. Measured after the prediction, the room was there (0.12 to 0.25 per person), but the map took about a fifteenth of it, and the person’s own model fitted on all their 4,000 single trials did no better. Those ceilings come from COCO photos while the labels are ImageNet photos, so part of the gap may be a change of image set. The predicted null happened, but not for the reason I gave. THINGS-EEG1 is the other open case: its labels are single trials too, yet the map was supported there, and I have no ceiling for its test people, so I don’t know how much room borrowing left.

Encoding is not the same as identifying

In fMRI I also looked at a second task, identification: given a brain response, which of 200 images was the person looking at? The model predicts a response for each of the 200 images, and we check if the closest prediction belongs to the right image. A model can be good at predicting responses and still be less good at picking the image, so I scored both. In the main fMRI test (NSD), the map combined with the person’s own model picked the right image 7.4 percentage points more often than borrowing, for all 8 people.

Figure 7 follows the same people while they give more and more labelled images, up to their whole recording. It uses features from DINOv2, a newer and stronger image network than the AlexNet of the main test, so all numbers are higher. Click a name in the legend to hide or show it. Each thin line is one person.

In Figure 7, with 500 images the map on borrowing is far ahead of the person’s own model. With more images the own model catches up, but even with the whole recording the map stays a little ahead, and adding the own model to it helps a bit more. Borrowing matters most when you have little data from the person, and it does not hurt when you have a lot.

NoteFor ML readers: why encoding and identification disagree

Encoding scores each output on its own, across images. Identification scores each image across all outputs at once: it picks, among 200 candidates, the image whose predicted pattern correlates best with the measured one. So identification also cares about the offsets and scales between vertices, which encoding ignores, and a correction can be better at one and worse at the other. NSD uses four fixed galleries of 200 images, drawn from its 872 shared test images.

To see where this comes from, I split the fMRI map into its two parts, in a development comparison on the eight NSD test people with AlexNet features and the test’s budgets. The map is “the own model inside the donors’ subspace” plus “anchored at borrowing”. Shrinking the same subspace model towards zero instead of towards borrowing encoded slightly better (0.314 against 0.302, for all eight people) but identified far fewer images (26% against 36%). So anchoring costs a little encoding and buys a lot of identification. The subspace itself barely matters for encoding: started from borrowing, the donor subspace, the features’ own principal directions and all the features came within 0.002 of each other, while random directions fell to 0.279.

No system won both tasks. Gu’s weighted ensemble trailed the map in encoding (by 0.045 to 0.096 in the three versions fitted), but its per-vertex version matched the map in identification. The map fitted on top of Gu’s ensemble gave the best encoding or the best identification, depending on how it was pooled, never both. The map anchored at borrowing is the simplest system that did well at both. Why anchoring helps identification is not isolated; it may lie in the offsets and scales it keeps across vertices.

One detail on scoring. Raw scores use the predictions as they are. Centred scores first subtract each output’s mean over the 200 test images, which uses unlabelled test responses, so they appear only where named, as in NSD’s identification test.

What I take from it

  • Write the test before you run it. My first idea failed its test. Without the protocol I would have kept the wrong story.
  • Try the simple thing first. A plain average of other people was stronger than the network I spent so long on.
  • Reproduce before you improve. Correct baselines made every later comparison clear.
  • Small and cheap can be enough. The final method is a few lines of linear algebra.
  • Say “not sure” when the data says “not sure”. The consumer EEG result is inconclusive, and it stays like that.

Limits

The EEG gains are small, a few hundredths in correlation. Each test that passed rests on one dataset, and no result has been repeated yet on a second group of fresh people. Some tests reused people that I had seen before (the follow-up on THINGS-EEG1 and the repeat on THINGS-EEG2), and I say this in each place. The parts about room, label noise and identification are development analyses, not prespecified tests.
