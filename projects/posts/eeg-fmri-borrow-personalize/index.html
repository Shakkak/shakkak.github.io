Borrow, then personalize

How I tried to predict a new person’s brain responses from a few hundred images, and what I learned on the way

EEG
fMRI
brain encoding

A walkthrough of my EEG and fMRI project: a big shared network lost to a simple average of other people, and a small personal map on top of that average became the main result.

November 6, 2026

Code and protocols on GitHub

The question

When you look at a picture, your brain answers with a pattern. We can record it with EEG (sensors on the head) or with fMRI (a scanner). An encoding model takes the image and predicts this pattern.

A good model for one person needs many hours of their recordings. A new person usually gives much less, maybe a few hundred labelled images. But we already have models of many other people. So my question was simple:

If I have only a few hundred images from a new person, what is the best way to use them, when models of other people already exist?

I did this work on free Kaggle notebooks. I had no GPU of my own, so every idea had to be cheap enough to run there.

Where I started: a big shared network

At the start I followed the trend in the field. Many recent papers train one network on many people and add a small part for each person. My plan was the same: a shared core for everyone, a small adapter for each new person, and later some extra ideas on top, like contrastive training across people.

Before building anything, I reproduced a published EEG encoding model and its baselines, and checked that my numbers matched the paper. This step was slow and boring, but later it saved me. Every new idea was compared with baselines that I knew were correct.

On THINGS-EEG2 (ten people) the shared network looked good. With 250 or 500 images from a new person it beat the baselines. I was happy. But there was a problem: I had used these same ten people while building the model. A model can look good on the people it was tuned on.

The test that changed the project

So I wrote the test down before running it. In a protocol, committed to git, I fixed the model, the people, the score, the margin a gain must clear, and the decision rule. Then I ran it once, on 24 people of a second EEG dataset (THINGS-EEG1) that no model had seen.

The shared network beat a model trained only on the person’s own few hundred images. But it lost to something much simpler: just average the predictions of the models of other people. I call this borrowing. The test said “rejected”, and I kept that result.

Use the menu above the chart to switch between the two comparisons. Each line is one person; coloured lines go up, grey lines go down. Hover a point to see the person.

Borrowing was better than the person’s own model for 22 of 24 people. The shared network was below borrowing for 18 of 24.

This was the most important moment of the project. I had spent a lot of time on the big network, and a plain average of other people beat it, on people it had never seen. If I had not written the test first, I could have searched for some setting where the network wins, and I would have believed it.

Borrow, then personalize

So I changed the direction. If borrowing is such a strong start, the new person’s few hundred images should not build a model from zero. They should correct the borrowed prediction.

The correction is small on purpose. It is a regularised linear map that mixes the borrowed prediction across EEG sensors (or fMRI vertices) in the way this one person needs. I call it the personal map. Two steps:

  1. Borrow. Average the predictions of encoders trained on other people. No labels of the new person needed.
  2. Personalize. Fit the small map from the new person’s labelled images, on top of that average.

Then I did the same as before: protocol first, one run, a fixed margin. On the 24 THINGS-EEG1 people the map improved on borrowing for 23 of them, in the early visual response. To be honest about it: this follow-up used the same 24 people as the first test, and I wrote it after I saw the first result.

Does it hold on other data?

One dataset is not enough. So I wrote new protocols and tested the same idea on four more datasets: a second lab EEG dataset, a consumer EEG headset, 7-tesla fMRI and single-trial 3-tesla fMRI. Each test was run once.

In the chart below every dot is one person. Above zero means the map was better than borrowing for that person. The red dashed line is the margin that the protocol said a gain must clear. Hover a dot for the person, or a diamond for the mean and its interval.

In short:

  • Lab EEG (THINGS-EEG1 and THINGS-EEG2): the map helped, for almost every person. On THINGS-EEG2 it cleared the margin over borrowing, but not over every other method in that test, so that test as a whole did not pass. THINGS-EEG2’s people were also the ones I used to build the baselines, so it is the weaker of the two.
  • Consumer EEG (Alljoined, 16 people): the gain went the same way, for 12 of 16 people, but did not clear the margin. The answer here is “not sure”, and I report it like that.
  • 7-tesla fMRI (NSD, 8 people): the map helped every person. Encoding accuracy went up by about a fifth.
  • Single-trial 3-tesla fMRI (NOD, 21 people): no gain. And this was the result I had predicted in the protocol, before the test. The next section says why.

Why it works sometimes and not always

The pattern became clear from the development work. The map can only fix what borrowing leaves on the table. It needs two things:

  1. Room. If borrowing is already close to the best that the noisy data allows, there is nothing left to gain.
  2. Clean labels. The map learns from the new person’s responses. If each response is very noisy, the map learns noise.

NOD records each image only once, on a 3-tesla scanner, so its labels are very noisy. That is why I predicted no gain there, and the test agreed.

The clean-label part I could check directly on THINGS-EEG2, where each image was shown several times. I trained the map on labels that averaged 1, 2 or 4 repetitions, and kept everything else the same. Use the menu above the chart to switch between two kinds of map and two label budgets.

With cleaner labels the gain grows, for the same people and the same number of images. So the few hundred images of a new person are worth more when each one is recorded well.

Encoding is not the same as identifying

In fMRI I also looked at a second task: given a brain response, which of 200 images was the person looking at? Here I found something I did not expect. The best model for predicting the response is not always the best model for picking the image. In the main fMRI test, the map combined with the person’s own model picked the right image 7.4 points more often than borrowing.

The chart below follows the same people while they give more and more labelled images, up to their whole recording. It uses stronger image features (DINOv2) than the main test, so all numbers are higher. Click a name in the legend to hide or show it. Each thin line is one person.

With 500 images the map on borrowing is far ahead of the person’s own model. With more images the own model catches up, but even with the whole recording the map stays a little ahead, and adding the own model to it helps a bit more. Borrowing matters most when you have little data from the person, and it does not hurt when you have a lot.

What I take from it

  • Write the test before you run it. My first idea failed its test. Without the protocol I would have kept the wrong story.
  • Try the simple thing first. A plain average of other people was stronger than the network I spent so long on.
  • Reproduce before you improve. Correct baselines made every later comparison clear.
  • Small and cheap can be enough. All of this ran on free Kaggle notebooks. The final method is a few lines of linear algebra.
  • Say “not sure” when the data says “not sure”. The consumer EEG result is inconclusive, and it stays like that.

Limits

The EEG gains are small, a few hundredths in correlation. Each confirmation used one dataset. Some tests reused people that I had seen before (the THINGS-EEG1 follow-ups and the THINGS-EEG2 replication), and I say this in each place. The parts about room, label noise and identification are development analyses, not prespecified tests.

Where to look

Everything is on GitHub: the code, the protocols of every test, the commit that holds each protocol before its run, and the result tables behind every figure. The charts on this page are built from those tables with a short script (data/prepare_data.py in this post’s source folder).
